# Japan

Japan chose not to digitise the bill of exchange. It created a new statutory claim, the electronically recorded monetary claim, kept by designated registries, and is now ending the use of paper promissory notes and cheques altogether.

## Law 

[Electronically Recorded Monetary Claims Act (電子記録債権法, Act No. 102 of 2007)](https://www.japaneselawtranslation.go.jp/en/laws/view/3173) — A monetary claim that comes into existence, and is transferred, only by a record in the register of a designated recording institution. It is a claim of its own kind, distinct from a bill under the Bills of Exchange Act (手形法).

[Financial Services Agency: designated electronic monetary claim recording institutions](https://www.fsa.go.jp/common/paper/2024/zentai/3-12-06.pdf) — Five institutions as of June 2025: Japan Electronic Monetary Claim Organization (MUFG group, 2009), SMBC (2010), Mizuho (2010), Densai.net (Japanese Bankers Association, 2013) and Tranzax (2016).

[Japanese Bankers Association: end of paper notes and cheques](https://www.zenginkyo.or.jp/kessai/news/21999/) — Clearing of paper promissory notes and cheques at the electronic clearing house ends on 31 March 2027; many banks stop issuing them on 30 September 2026. Since 1 January 2026 the amended subcontracting payment law no longer allows promissory notes as a means of payment ([METI](https://www.chusho.meti.go.jp/koukai/kenkyukai/kigyo_torihiki/r7_003/005.pdf)).

## Systems and projects

[Densai.net (株式会社全銀電子債権ネットワーク)](https://www.zenginkyo.or.jp/kessai/news/20427/) — The largest recording institution, connected to practically all Japanese banks; supports split transfer of claims. (The operator's site, densai.net, was not reachable when this page was prepared.)

**Compared with our bills:** Japan replaced the bill with a register entry made by a designated operator, so the questions of writing, signature and possession do not arise. Our bills keep the bill and answer those questions with the public register: the entry is made by the parties themselves, by signing transactions, and the register has no operator. The Japanese claim is held through a bank account by an identified party as deferred trade credit; our bill is held by a bearer at sight. Japan's path shows what a state can do with a designated registry; ours shows what the parties can do without one.
